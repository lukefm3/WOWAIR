\title{WOW Factor: Measuring Labor Loss from a Negative Tourism Supply Shock in Iceland}
%\author{Luke Miller}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle
\clearpage

\begin{abstract}
Tourism-dependent economies face substantial risks when international visitor flows are disrupted, yet evidence on the short-run labor market effects of sudden tourism supply shocks remains limited. This paper exploits the unexpected bankruptcy of WOW Air, a low-cost airline, in 2019 as a natural experiment that sharply curtailed international tourism to Iceland. Using a Bayesian Structural Time Series model, I estimate labor market outcomes relative to a counterfactual in which the airline did not exit. I find that WOW Air’s collapse reduced international passenger arrivals by more than 20\% and tourism employment by 8\% over the subsequent nine months, relative to the counterfactual, including a 4\% year-over-year decline in tourism jobs. Excluding air transport, tourism employment declined by 6.6\% relative to the counterfactual. At the national level, unemployment was nearly 50\% higher than would otherwise have been expected. These results highlight the vulnerability of tourism-dependent economies to sudden transport disruptions.
\\ 
\\
\textbf{Keywords:}  tourism employment; air transport; tourism supply shock; economic vulnerability; counterfactual analysis;  natural experiment
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:Introduction}
The COVID-19 pandemic revealed the acute vulnerabilities of tourism-dependent economies, with international shutdowns exposing the fragility of over-reliance on a single sector. \citet{duro_2021_covid19} show that regions with higher tourism concentrations experienced disproportionate exposure to pandemic losses. \citet{jczmyk_2023_impact} identify how tourist behaviors and choices shifted in response to health risks and uncertainty. \citet{yeh_2020_tourism} and \citet{kreiner_2020_national} evaluate optimal recovery strategies, while \citet{fotiadis_2021_the} and \citet{kare_2020_impact} provide forecasts of tourism impacts and recovery trajectories under varying conditions. \citet{sigala_2020_tourism} reflects more broadly on the changed research landscape, emphasizing the unprecedented scope of the pandemic and the challenge of generalizing from its impacts. Taken together, this literature highlights how tourism reliance can create vulnerabilities for associated labor markets, but also how unique the pandemic was in both scale and global reach. At the same time, it leaves unresolved the question of replicability: while COVID-19 exposed the sector’s vulnerabilities in an extraordinary way, the shock was shaped by many confounding factors beyond tourism itself, making it difficult to entirely isolate effects or anticipate the size of future disruptions. This uncertainty underscores the importance of identifying more tractable episodes to guide policy responses aimed at mitigating the labor market and fiscal risks of sudden tourism contractions.

Beyond the pandemic, structural reliance on foreign tourism may create inherent economic vulnerabilities due to dependence on external visitor flows. \citet{nguyen_2021_the} find that a greater concentration of international tourism increases economic vulnerability, while \citet{canh_2020_domestic} show that domestic tourism can reduce it. \citet{wang_2022_international} identify a threshold effect: at lower income levels international tourism heightens vulnerability, while at higher incomes it promotes diversification. However, these studies rely on international panel regressions where vulnerability is measured through the Economic Vulnerability Index (EVI) \citep{feindouno_2016_the}, rather than observed economic responses. Because the EVI includes export concentration as a component, and international tourism is classified as an export, this creates endogeneity concerns. Moreover, the index captures exposure to shocks but not resilience to withstand them. Meanwhile, the astounding resilience of the industry in response to crises that dissuade visits, is highlighted in a complementary set of studies. \citet{saleh_2011_do} document that tourism shocks in Thailand are transitory, and \citet{yucel_2020_are} extend this finding to the top 20 tourism-receiving countries, with demand reverting once structural breaks are accounted for. \citet{morakabati_2019_a} shows that, following the Arab Spring, regional international tourism receipts recovered faster than the broader economy. These studies illustrate both the fragility and resilience of the sector, but provide little causal evidence of how sudden supply disruptions affect labor markets or how policy may mitigate their impacts. 

Tourism has also been closely linked to economic growth. \citet{gwenhure_2017_tourism} synthesize this literature into four hypotheses: that tourism causes growth, that growth causes tourism, that the relationship is bidirectional, or that there is no causal link. Studies provide evidence in support of each, though the tourism-led growth channel receives somewhat stronger backing overall. Within island contexts, results are less conclusive. \citet{roudi_2018_new} find bi-directionality between tourism and growth in small island developing states, while \citet{kumar_2021_tourism} show country-dependent effects in nations in the pacific. Using an augmented Solow growth framework, Using panel data from 19 countries, \citet{seetanah_2011_assessing} find that tourism contributes more significantly to economic growth in island economies than in other developing and developed countries. At a regional scale, \citet{faber_2019_tourism} find that tourism in Mexico produces strong local employment and GDP gains through manufacturing spillovers, though these effects are muted at the national level. This body of work underscores the importance of tourism for growth and employment, but offers limited insight into contractionary episodes or how labor market rigidities might constrain adjustment when shocks occur.


These strands of research establish that tourism shocks are closely tied to labor outcomes, that tourism-dependent economies face structural vulnerabilities - yet rebound quickly, and that tourism plays a central role in growth. Low-cost carriers (LCCs) provide a further dimension to the analysis, as WOW Air itself operated under this model. Low-Cost Carriers (LCCs) have been shown to play a unique role in shaping tourism supply. \citet{alves_2017_price} describe how LCCs operate as price-discriminating complements, targeting more price-sensitive markets. Case studies across Saudi Arabia, Poland, Spain, New Zealand, and Italy demonstrate that LCC entry expands demand by creating new, previously uncontested markets \citep{alsumairi_2017_a, pulina_2010_have, rey_2011_effect, hongtsui_2017_does, verarebollo_2009_spread}. By artificially inflating arrivals through lower fares and novel routes, LCCs increase reliance on an expanded visitor base that may vanish if those routes are withdrawn. Their sudden contraction therefore amplifies the consequences of supply shocks, as other carriers are often unable to replicate the same capacity or reach the same markets. At the same time, because LCCs create new demand rather than reshuffling existing passengers, their exit provides a sharp and isolated test of how supply-side shocks cascade into labor markets. 
\begin{sloppypar}
This paper addresses the lack of causal evidence on how sudden supply-side shocks propagate through labor markets by exploiting the unexpected 2019 bankruptcy of WOW Air in Iceland as a natural experiment. Following the 2008 financial crisis, Iceland promoted tourism as a central growth strategy, with arrivals quadrupling between 2010 and 2018. WOW Air, founded in 2011 as a low-cost carrier, was central to this expansion, driving demand through low fares and expanded routes. Its closure in March 2019 produced an immediate contraction in arrivals, exposing the extent to which employment and output had come to rely on sustained inflows of foreign visitors. The setting is an optimal case: a high-income island economy, heavy reliance on a single carrier, and the sharp, non-substitutable cutoff in international passengers created by the firm’s abrupt collapse. 

Using a Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) model~\citep{brodersen_2015_inferring}, I construct counterfactual outcomes to estimate the causal impacts of this supply shock. This approach isolates the disruption caused by the airline’s collapse, providing rare causal evidence of how firm-level contractions can ripple through labor markets in small, tourism-dependent economies. The analysis demonstrates that tourism supply shocks can quickly spread through employment and public revenues, underscoring the risks of over-specialization in tourism and the fiscal pressures created by sudden drops in international visitors. These dynamics highlight the need for resilience planning and targeted policies to mitigate the impacts of airline or carrier exits in similarly dependent contexts.
\end{sloppypar}





\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Choice of Controls} 

Greenland, Denmark, Norway, and the Faroe Islands are used as control groups to estimate Iceland’s counterfactual. These countries were selected based on geographic proximity and similarities in size, tourism concentration, seasonality, and export reliance, as summarized in Table \ref{tab:summary_table}. Sweden, Ireland, and the United Kingdom were also considered, but ultimately excluded due to larger differences in size, economic diversification, and geography. While Greenland and the Faroe Islands are autonomous territories within the Kingdom of Denmark, Denmark itself is included as a separate control due to its role as a primary regional trading partner whose economic fluctuations often co-vary with those of Iceland and its territories. As shown in Appendix \ref{sec:Robustness}, the estimated impacts are robust to alternative specifications of the control group.

\subsection{Dataset}

I assemble a monthly cross-national time-series dataset of tourism and economic indicators from national statistical agencies.\footnote{Iceland: \textit{hagstofa.is} (2024); Faroe Islands: \textit{hagstova.fo} (2024); Greenland: \textit{bank.stat.gl} (2024); Denmark: \textit{statbank.dk} (2024); Norway: \textit{ssb.no} (2024).} Nordic regional data was collected from \textit{nordicstatistics.org}.\footnote{\textit{nordicstatistics.org} (2024).} Where official monthly data were unavailable, I supplemented with the World Bank Open Data Portal\footnote{\textit{data.worldbank.org} (2024).} and The Global Economy.\footnote{\textit{theglobaleconomy.com} (2024).} Indicators were excluded if coverage was incomplete across the pre- or post-intervention period, if they were linear combinations of other variables, or if they were subject to high measurement noise (e.g., survey data with small samples).  


\subsection{Data Availability}\label{sec:Data Availability}

Limitations in uniform cross-country data availability shaped the empirical approach. The synthetic control method of \citet{abadie_2003_the} requires identical outcome variables across treatment and control groups, a condition not met here. Our dataset's reported indicators vary in scope, frequency, and level of disaggregation across countries. For example, Greenland and the Faroe Islands provide relatively few monthly indicators, and those that are available are often defined differently from Iceland’s series. In some cases, measures are reported only in aggregate form, while in others they are disaggregated by sector, age, or region, reducing comparability across countries. For instance, Iceland reports both total and sector-specific employment and wage measures, including total employees, employees in tourism, and employees in accommodation and food services, whereas Greenland provides only an aggregate employment figure without any breakdown by sector or industry. Even so, indicators from control countries may still serve as informative predictors of related outcomes in Iceland (e.g., passenger arrivals in Greenland may co-vary with hotel check-ins in Iceland). These information asymmetries are summarized in Table \ref{tab:information_asymmetry}.

 As discussed further in Section \ref{sec:methods}, I group available variables into topic areas and use them as covariate groups in the model’s calibration. Because the Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) framework is able to self-select predictive covariates, results remain consistent whether I include the full set or only the most relevant indicators. For example, I use the Tourism \& Transportation Topic area from Table \ref{tab:information_asymmetry} to predict tourism outcomes. But if I also include additional information about local fuel use and national accounting, only the most relevant covariates are ultimately selected (depicted in Table \ref{tab:choice_control_coeffs}).

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Empirical Design}

The objective is to estimate the effect of the WOW Air bankruptcy on Iceland’s outcomes by comparing observed series to a counterfactual path that would have occurred in its absence. Formally, the causal effect at time $t$ is the difference between the observed outcome for Iceland ($Y_{I,t}$) and the unobserved counterfactual ($Y_{N,t}$), where the WOW Air bankruptcy did not happen. Since $Y_{N,t}$ is not observed, it must be estimated from a model informed by Iceland’s own pre-trends and by contemporaneous outcomes in unaffected countries.

I implement a Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) model following \citet{brodersen_2015_inferring}. Let the pre-intervention training period be $t=1,\dots,n$, where $n$ is January 2019. The post-intervention forecasting period begins with $t=n+3,\dots,m$, where the two-month buffer $(n+1,n+2)$ accounts for WOW Air’s gradual route reductions before its final bankruptcy mid-March 2019. The counterfactual outcome for Iceland is modeled as:
\begin{align}
Y_t &= \mu_t + \tau_t + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} x_{j,t}\beta_j + \varepsilon_t, \quad t = 1,\dots,m \label{eq:obs} \\
\mu_{t+1} &= \mu_t + \delta_t + \eta_{\mu,t}, \quad t = 1,\dots,n \label{eq:level} \\
\delta_{t+1} &= \delta_t + \eta_{\delta,t}, \quad t = 1,\dots,n \label{eq:slope} \\
\tau_{t+1} &= -\sum_{k=1}^{S-1}\tau_{t-k} + \eta_{\tau,t}, \quad S=12 \label{eq:season}
\end{align}

Equation \eqref{eq:obs} is the observation equation. The observed outcome (e.g., international passenger departures, tourism employment) is expressed as the sum of latent components plus noise, with contemporaneous controls $x_{j,t}$ from unaffected countries $j \in \mathcal{J}$. Equation \eqref{eq:level} defines a local level $\mu_t$, representing Iceland’s underlying trend. Equation \eqref{eq:slope} introduces a local slope $\delta_t$, which allows the trend to change over time. Equation \eqref{eq:season} adds a seasonal component $\tau_t$ with $S=12$ months, capturing Iceland’s highly seasonal tourism cycle. Gaussian disturbances $\varepsilon_t, \eta_{\mu,t}, \eta_{\delta,t}, \eta_{\tau,t}$ are mutually independent.

Control selection is handled via spike-and-slab priors, which induce sparsity and allow the model to include only those covariates that improve predictive fit in the pre-treatment period. Intuitively, the “spike” shrinks irrelevant coefficients toward zero while the “slab” allows nonzero values for genuinely predictive controls, letting the model probabilistically switch covariates on or off during estimation. This prevents overfitting while highlighting the controls that truly drive Iceland’s pre-2019 tourism trajectory. Figure~\ref{fig:coefficients_arrivals_plot} summarizes the posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) from the primary model, indicating which covariates were most consistently selected across posterior draws and their coefficient magnitudes.

\paragraph{Model Calibration}
To implement the model, I use the \texttt{CausalImpact} package in R \citep{brodersen_2015_inferring}. Being a flexible tool, it allows for numerous user calibrating inputs. The choice of pre-intervention window reflects a trade-off between having longer training periods and ensuring rich covariate coverage. Data availability improves substantially after 2014 (see Figure \ref{fig:recent_information_bias}), so I test specifications beginning in each year from 2000–2018, with treatment fixed in April 2019. Figure \ref{fig:time_calibration} reports cumulative impacts across these specifications. The estimates vary somewhat by training start date but consistently indicate a large causal reduction in airline departures. Given structural changes in Iceland’s economy toward tourism beginning around 2012,\footnote{WOW Air began operations in May 2012; see \citet{diebold_1987_structural} for discussion of structural breaks.} I adopt 2014 as the start year for the main analysis. This balances adequate time-series coverage with robustness and placebo validity. Across all training windows (2000–2018), the mean cumulative impact is approximately –487,000 passengers and the median –458,000, with the 2014 specification yielding an estimated –538,000 passengers.

The forecasting window begins in April 2019 ($t=n+3$) and continues through December 2019 ($m$). Excluding February and March ($n+1,n+2$) is essential because these months represent a transitory phase when WOW Air first reduced routes and then ceased operations mid-March, making them unsuitable for isolating the causal shock. Covariates $x_{j,t}$ are drawn from Nordic and near-Nordic economies, selected from the topic areas summarized in Table \ref{tab:information_asymmetry}, ensuring economic relevance and avoiding implausible predictors. Seasonality is modeled explicitly with a monthly component ($S=12$), reflecting the strong peaks and troughs in Arctic tourism activity. All other hyperparameters follow the defaults recommended by \citet{brodersen_2015_inferring}.

This section begins by analyzing the impact of the WOW Air closure on passenger travel, which provides both the clearest channel through which the bankruptcy affected the economy and an important test of the empirical method. If the modeling approach cannot recover the well-documented reduction in international passengers, confidence in the downstream labor results would be limited. Conversely, demonstrating that the model captures the contraction in passenger flows validates the method and provides a necessary foundation for interpreting the labor outcomes.

Figure \ref{fig:primary_arrivals_plot} shows the estimated counterfactual number of passengers compared to observed departures as well as the monthly and cumulative differences. In the nine months following the bankruptcy, approximately 2.07 million passengers departed Iceland, compared to a counterfactual estimate of 2.60 million passengers in a synthetic Iceland where the bankruptcy did not occur. This implies about 534,000 fewer passengers than would have been expected, a reduction of roughly 20.5\%. Figure \ref{fig:fig_table_grouped} shows the same results broken down by passenger citizenship. Foreign visitors account for the majority of this decline: relative losses were 4\% larger for foreigners than for Icelandic residents, and in absolute terms the contraction in foreign passengers was nearly four times greater. These patterns align with the expectation that WOW Air’s exit would disproportionately affect international tourism flows with strong implications for lost export revenue.

Departures remained consistently below expected levels by roughly 15–25\% throughout 2019, with little sign of recovery even toward the end of the year. While this pattern is not formally tested, the results suggest that the lost capacity was not easily substituted by other carriers during this period. This interpretation is consistent with the broader literature on low-cost carriers, which emphasizes that such airlines often stimulate their own demand and serve distinct market segments not readily filled by legacy carriers \citep{alsumairi_2017_a,pulina_2010_have,rey_2011_effect,hongtsui_2017_does,verarebollo_2009_spread,khan_2019_evolving}. Although exploratory, the persistence of the gap indicates that WOW Air played a distinctive role in sustaining international travel demand to and from Iceland, with meaningful implications for the country’s tourism-driven export sector.

Because subsequent results build directly on the finding that tourism supply was substantially affected by the bankruptcy and because I utilize the same model and calibration for subsequent outcomes, it is essential that this estimate be robust and credible. To this end, I implement a broad set of placebo and specification checks, detailed in the Appendix. Placebo tests apply the same treatment to outcomes or countries that should be unaffected, providing a check against false positives and helping guard against type II errors. These include assigning the bankruptcy shock to other Nordic countries, to unrelated Icelandic outcomes such as fish landings, and to pre-bankruptcy periods. Only the pre-bankruptcy placebos show notable treatment effects, likely capturing WOW Air’s expansion rather than a true shock, though these estimates are much less precise than the true bankruptcy effect, with placebo results significant at roughly the 1\% level compared to 0.02\% (p=0.0002) for the actual bankruptcy. Robustness tests varying the control countries used, covariate information included, and model specifications all yield consistent results, and comparison with a two-way fixed effects model shows estimates of similar magnitude, underscoring the reliability of the BSTS approach. Together, these tests reinforce confidence that the observed decline in passengers is causally attributable to the bankruptcy.

\paragraph{Sectoral employment effects.}
The panel Employed Persons By Job Sector in Figure \ref{fig:national_labor_grouped}a shows that overall tourism employment fell by 8.1\%, or roughly 6,000 jobs per month, relative to the counterfactual. These values represent deviations from the projected growth path rather than true job losses, likely reflecting a combination of foregone employment that would likely have occurred had pre-shock trends continued and actual jobs lost. Air transport experienced the steepest decline in employment at 25.8\% ($-1,540$ jobs), and the broader transport sector fell by 16.5\% ($-2,650$ jobs). For context, WOW Air employed between 1,000 and 1,100 workers at the time of its bankruptcy, and in the counterfactual scenario, continued growth would likely have been expected. Additional labor losses appeared in accommodation ($-10.5\%$) and food \& beverage ($-8.4\%$), though these estimates carry greater statistical uncertainty. Collectively, these results confirm that employment reductions were felt deeply within tourism-related industries.

\paragraph{National unemployment response.}
The sharp contractions in tourism-facing sectors also led to an aggregate rise in national unemployment. Figure \ref{fig:national_labor_grouped}b shows the monthly differences between actual unemployment levels and the synthetic at the national level, while the National Level Indicators panel of Figure \ref{fig:national_labor_grouped}a presents the corresponding estimated effects. The number of unemployed persons increased by 48.5\% relative to the counterfactual, equivalent to about 2,600 additional unemployed workers per month. The unemployment rate rose by 1.3 percentage points, or 49.5\% relative to the counterfactual. These are substantial shifts within a labor force of roughly 200,000 workers, yet they are substantially lower than the projected 6,000 employee decrease in tourism, indicating a likely shift across sectors. As mentioned, because this method measures differences relative to a modeled counterfactual rather than tracking actual losses. Consequently, this approach cannot test for cross-sectoral labor shifts, especially as non-tourism employment and tourism employment are likely endogenous, as the expansion of tourism can contribute to wider job creation across the economy.

Yet, when the effects of the bankruptcy on employment is measured net of tourism, the evidence becomes less precise ($p = 0.0141$). Total non-tourism employment grew relative to the counterfactual, though its composition differs. In simple terms, employment in tourism fell by roughly $3.09\%$ year-over-year (approximately $-4,000$ jobs), while employment outside of tourism rose by about $3.93\%$ ($+2,500$ jobs).


\paragraph{Taxable wages.}
Employment losses are also reflected in taxable wage data, though the declines are less pronounced at the national level.  The \textbf{Taxable Wages By Job Sector} panel of Table \ref{fig:fig_table_grouped}a  reveals transport wages (inclusive of air transport) fell by 15.6\% (–1.56 billion ISK monthly), while accommodation wages fell by 7.0\%. At the aggregate level, national wages fell by over 2\%, but without statistical significance. The lack of significance could reflect the small scale of tourism employment relative to national employment, or it could speak to the flexibility and substition of hourly labor whereby employees leaving tourism find similar work quickly. Additionally, it could be reflective of unemployment benefits which may have cushioned part of the income loss.  

The magnitude of the labor market response is partly explained by Iceland’s high degree of specialization in tourism employment leading into 2019. As shown in Table \ref{tab:specialization}, the share of employees in tourism nearly doubled from 7.8\% in 2010 to 15.0\% in 2018. Sub-sectors such as accommodation (+141\%), tour services (+240\%), and air transport (+91\%) grew especially rapidly. This concentration left the labor market highly exposed to a sudden contraction in arrivals. The WOW Air bankruptcy therefore not only reduced employment in directly affected sectors but also exposed the vulnerability created by the preceding decade of rapid specialization. While Iceland’s degree of tourism specialization may appear unusually high, significantly higher concentrations of touri9sm employment are observed elsewhere, increasing the generalizability of these results. Across the Caribbean, travel and tourism comprise a dominant share of total employment, peaking at 90.7\% in Antigua and Barbuda in 2019, while all of the ten most tourism-dependent economies surpass 50\%.\footnote{Original data from the World Travel and Tourism Council (2019). Available at: \href{https://www.weforum.org/stories/2020/08/destinations-rely-most-on-tourism-travel/}{\textit{weforum.org}}.} These parallels highlight how economies heavily reliant on tourism face amplified labor market risks during shocks to international mobility, underscoring the importance of diversification in sustaining economic resilience.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
The collapse of WOW Air illustrates how a seemingly narrow, firm-specific event can trigger broader economic disruptions in a small, tourism-dependent economy. The shock spread quickly through the labor market, with consequences significant enough to be visible in aggregate national indicators.  

That WOW Air was a low-cost carrier is particularly notable. Although often viewed as peripheral, such carriers can underpin large volumes of activity by creating new routes and demand that are not easily replaced when withdrawn. This case shows that their exit can reverberate widely, affecting employment, fiscal revenues, and economic stability.  

Beyond documenting realized vulnerability, the study contributes causal evidence to a literature often limited to descriptive indices or correlations between tourism and growth. It highlights how quickly international travel shocks can translate into labor market dislocations and fiscal pressures, underscoring the importance for policymakers of recognizing the risks of over-specialization, strengthening resilience planning, and considering targeted support mechanisms for economies reliant on sustained visitor inflows.

\subsubsection{Treatment on Untreated Country}\label{sec:Placebo2}

Placebo treatments applied to control countries show that the estimated impacts are not replicable outside of Iceland (Table \ref{tab:complete_placebo_panel}, Panel Impact of Bankruptcy on Other Countries). When the 2019 bankruptcy shock is imposed on the Faroe Islands, Denmark, or Norway, the resulting impacts are small in magnitude and statistically insignificant. Greenland represents the exception, with an estimated relative effect of around -7.75\% that is marginally significant. However, given the small scale of the Greenland economy and limited availability of monthly indicators, this may be attributable to data volatility rather than a genuine treatment effect. These findings reinforce that the large and persistent decline in Icelandic passenger volumes is not an artifact of the donor pool construction.

\subsubsection{Treatment Years Prior}\label{sec:Placebo3}

When the bankruptcy shock is assigned to earlier years (2017 or 2018), the model produces statistically significant but smaller effects (Table \ref{tab:complete_placebo_panel}, Panel Impact of 2019 Bankruptcy on Iceland for Years Prior). This likely reflects WOW Air’s rapid expansion during those years being treated as an artificial “shock.” While such findings suggest the design can flag structural breaks during periods of growth, the strength of statistical support differs markedly: Placebo effects in earlier years are detected at the 5–10\% significance level, whereas the actual 2019 bankruptcy effect is identified at $p = 0.0002$, representing an effect roughly five to ten times more statistically significant than those observed in earlier periods ($p = 0.015$). This contrast provides further evidence that the observed impacts in 2019 represent a true disruption rather than a modeling artifact. To address residual concerns over time trends, I also present in the Appendix two alternative specifications of the time dynamics, both of which continue to yield a significant estimated impact of the bankruptcy on Icelandic tourism.


\subsection{Robustness Checks}\label{sec:Robustness}
Another advantage of the Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) model is its flexibility and the ability to test robustness through alternative specifications. Because there are seemingly limitless combinations of model calibrations, I focus on the main finding - predicted total departures from Iceland - and present three robustness exercises: (1) varying the choice of control countries, (2) varying the set of covariates, and (3) estimating a counterfactual without time-series components. We can consider these a test of Type 1 Error. Across all tests, the estimated passenger loss remains large and significant, providing confidence in the primary results.  

\subsubsection{Control Country Selection}\label{sec:robustness1}

First, I vary the set of control countries to ensure that results are not driven by over-specification, or by idiosyncratic behavior in a self-selected control group. Across alternative combinations of Greenland, Faroe Islands, Denmark, and Norway, the estimated relative impact remains between –20.3\% and –28.7\%, with p-values below 0.0002 for all specifications. This suggests the results are not sensitive to which countries are included as controls. See Table \ref{tab:choice_control_Countries}.

\subsubsection{Counterfactual Without Time-Series Components}\label{sec:robustnes3}

Finally, I estimate a simplified specification that removes all time-series and seasonality components, leaving an OLS-style regression similar to dynamic regressions proposed by \citet{banerjee_2007_modeling}. Using this approach we are predicting the outcome based solely off the relationship of the outcome variable with indicators in the control countries. 

The simplified ordinary least squares (OLS) model abstracts from the time-series dynamics of the Bayesian specification by omitting the latent level $(\mu_t)$ and seasonal $(\tau_t)$ components. The counterfactual outcome for Iceland is therefore modeled as:
\[
Y_t = \beta_0 + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} x_{j,t}\beta_j + \varepsilon_t, \quad j \neq i
\]
where $Y_t$ denotes the observed outcome (e.g., international passenger departures), and $x_{j,t}$ are contemporaneous covariates from unaffected countries $j \in \mathcal{J}$.

In this specification, the set of covariates $x$ is restricted to tourism-related indicators as defined in Table \ref{tab:information_asymmetry}. Monthly, cumulative, and relative differences are computed in the same manner as in the primary model. \subsection{Two-Way Fixed Effects Comparison}\label{sec:DID_Regression}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}

As a benchmark, I also estimate a simple two-way fixed effects regression, while noting that the parallel trends assumption is unlikely to hold in this context. The purpose of this exercise is not to provide a definitive estimate, but rather to illustrate the limitations of a conventional Difference-in-Differences design and the relative advantages of the BSTS counterfactual. \\

The model specification is:  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log(Y_{it}) = & \ \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} \cdot Treat_{i} + \beta_{2} \cdot (Post_{t} \times Treat_{i}) \\
& + \beta_{3} \cdot CountryFE_{i} + \beta_{4} \cdot YearFE_{t} + \beta_{5} \cdot MonthFE_{t} + \epsilon_{it}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(Y_{it}\) is the log of arrivals for country \(i\) at time \(t\). \(Post_{t}\) is an indicator equal to 1 for the period April–December 2019 and 0 otherwise. \(Treat_{i}\) identifies exposure to the bankruptcy shock (or placebo shocks, when applied to control countries). The specification includes country, year, and month fixed effects. \\

To explore sensitivity to the pre-intervention window, I estimate this model with varying lengths of pre-period data (Table \ref{tab:DID}). Results are unstable: as additional pre-period years are included, the estimated treatment effect for Iceland not only declines in magnitude but becomes positive. When applied as placebos to the four control countries, the model even produces significant negative “effects” for Norway and Denmark. These inconsistencies highlight the difficulty of applying a two-way fixed effects approach in the presence of strong pre-trends, such as the rapid growth of Icelandic tourism. By contrast, the BSTS model leverages prior time-series information and covariates to provide a more credible counterfactual when parallel trends cannot be assumed.
